Part Two — The Revolution

When American retailers wanted nothing to do with video games, Nintendo refused to call its product a game console at all — a decision that began a revolution.

OriginsFrom Famicom to NES

The system began in Japan as the Family Computer, or "Famicom," released on July 15, 1983. After it dominated the Japanese market, Nintendo set its sights on the United States — the very market that had just collapsed.

Disguising a Console

Because "video game" had become toxic to retailers, Nintendo of America rebranded everything. The machine became a sleek grey box that loaded cartridges from the front like a VCR, resembling a home appliance. Cartridges became "Game Paks," the console the "Control Deck," and it shipped with a robot accessory and light gun to look like high-tech gear.

LaunchThe Test Market Gamble

  • July 1983 — Famicom launches in Japan.
  • October 18, 1985 — NES test-launches in New York City (~100,000 systems).
  • February 1986 — Test market expands to Los Angeles.
  • September 27, 1986 — Nationwide U.S. release.

The New York launch was a calculated risk: to reassure nervous retailers, Nintendo reportedly offered buy-back guarantees on unsold systems. It worked, and the NES expanded city by city across the country.

The GamesWhy People Believed Again

Hardware alone could not rebuild trust; the games had to prove the medium was worth returning to. The NES delivered landmark titles that became enduring franchises: Super Mario Bros. (1985), The Legend of Zelda (1986), Metroid (1986), and Dragon Quest (1986). With richer worlds and tighter design than the crash-era games, they gave players a real reason to believe again.

Yet this revolution rested on an invisible foundation: a system of rules ensuring the failures of 1983 could never repeat, explored in Reform.
