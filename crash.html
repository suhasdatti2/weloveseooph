To understand why the NES mattered, you must first see the ruins it was built upon. By 1983, the American home video game market was collapsing under its own weight.

ContextA Market Floods Itself

In the early 1980s, home video games were booming. After the success of the Atari 2600, dozens of companies rushed consoles and cartridges to market with little oversight. Shelves filled with broken, repetitive titles, and buyers had no way to tell a good game from a bad one beforehand.

Notorious flops such as the rushed E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial became symbols of the collapse, piling up unsold until stores dumped them into bargain bins and shoppers came to see video games as worthless.

These numbers tell the story of a near-total wipeout. Major companies posted enormous losses, and much of the press declared the video game a passing fad.

CausesWhy the Industry Failed

Three failures drove the collapse. Market saturation put too many consoles and games before the same buyers. A lack of quality control let anyone publish a cartridge, flooding shelves with broken titles. Together these destroyed consumer confidence: burned shoppers stopped buying, and retailers stopped stocking games.

This was the wreckage Nintendo studied. Every reform later built into the NES was a direct reaction to the failures of 1983 — the foundation of the revolution that followed.
